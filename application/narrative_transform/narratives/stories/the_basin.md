# The Basin

The mule path ended at a shrine cut into black rock. Diego left a candle there—not from faith, but because the woman who sold it to him in the last village had looked at him like she was counting how many days he had left. The wax was cheap, the wick already crooked. He lit it anyway and watched it gutter in the wind before continuing up.

The basin opened below him an hour later: terraced fields stepping down from the ridgeline, a cluster of stone and thatch buildings around a plaza, and beyond it all a stream that caught the afternoon light like hammered silver. No walls. No watchtowers. Just the mountains themselves, ringing everything, close enough that he could see individual rocks on their flanks.

The guest house sat beside the canal. A woman was sweeping the doorstep when he arrived, her broom made from bundled grass that left neat parallel lines in the dust.

"You're late," she said in Spanish. Not accusatory. Just observing.

"I didn't know I was expected."

"The bell rang this morning. Two tolls. Council meets when someone new comes up the path." She leaned the broom against the wall. "You eat with everyone else. Water's from the canal, cold year-round. Don't drink from the stream directly unless you want to spend three days bent over."

Inside, the room was small and clean: a sleeping mat rolled against the wall, a wooden chest, a shuttered window that looked down toward the plaza. No lock on the door. No lock on the chest either, he noticed, though he checked.

That evening he ate in the common hall—a long building open on one side, its roof supported by carved posts that someone had taken time with, spirals and birds worked into the grain. The food came from a pot large enough to boil a sheep whole: quinoa and potatoes and something leafy he didn't recognize. The woman who ladled it out gave him the same portion as everyone else.

He tried to pay her with a silver real.

She looked at the coin, then at him, then laughed—not unkindly. "Keep your little moon. You'll need it when you leave."

"For what?"

"Whatever you'll want down there." She gestured vaguely at the mountains, meaning everything beyond them.

The council met under a pepper tree by the church. Diego attended because someone rang the bell twice and a boy came to the guest house to fetch him. He sat on a bench worn smooth by other backs and listened to them discuss whether to widen the canal next year or repair the guest house roof first. They spoke in Spanish when they looked at him, switching to something else—Quechua maybe, or older—when they forgot he was there.

An old man with white hair and hands like knotted rope asked him where he'd come from.

"Potosí," Diego said. "Before that, Lima."

"Long way."

"I'm a trader. Or I was. The partnerships dissolved."

This was partly true. The partnerships had dissolved because Diego had borrowed against silver he didn't have to buy goods he couldn't sell, and when the debts came due he'd walked away from his lodgings in the middle of the night with everything he owned in a canvas sack. But "dissolved" sounded better.

"What do you trade?" the old man asked.

"Whatever moves. Cloth, tools, sometimes letters for people who can't write."

The old man nodded. "We send wool down once a month. And bowls—carved ones, the good hard wood. Someone meets us in Quito, takes them to sell. We bring back iron, salt, paper for the priest."

"No coin comes back up?"

"Some. Not much use here." The old man gestured at the plaza, the fields, the mountains. "Can't eat silver. Can't weave with it. Mostly we keep what we need and share what we don't."

Diego looked around the council. Fourteen people, maybe fifteen. Calloused hands, good wool cloaks, faces that suggested none of them had missed many meals. No jewelry except for a few women wearing small silver crosses. No obvious hierarchy except age, and even that seemed negotiable based on who was talking.

"You don't have a headman? A governor?"

"Council decides. Everyone sends someone when the bell rings."

"What if someone doesn't come?"

"Then they don't get a say." The old man smiled slightly. "Hasn't happened yet."

They assigned him to field work—two days a week, rotating between the upper and lower terraces. The work was straightforward: weeding, turning soil, hauling water when the canal gates were closed for repairs. He worked beside a young woman who spoke very little and an older man who spoke constantly, mostly about weather and soil composition and which seeds his grandmother had saved through the bad years.

On his third day, Diego found a stone by the edge of the field. Dull yellow, heavy for its size, with tiny flecks that caught the light. He picked it up, turned it over, felt its weight.

The older man glanced at it. "Doorstop. Or a sinker if you're fixing nets for the pond."

"It's gold."

"Probably. Lots of it in the rocks up there." He pointed at the ridge. "Stream brings it down sometimes after the rains."

"And you use it for doorstops?"

The man shrugged. "What else? Can't eat it. Doesn't hold an edge. Too soft for tools." He went back to weeding.

Diego put the stone in his pocket.

That night he lay awake in the guest house, fingers closing around the stone, mind calculating. In Potosí, a piece this size would buy three months of lodging. In Lima, maybe six. In Quito, someone would weigh it, argue about purity, eventually hand over enough silver to book passage on a ship.

He got up, lit the candle stub he'd saved, and examined the stone more carefully. Definitely gold. Not pure—nothing ever was—but pure enough. And if there was one stone, there were others.

The next morning he walked the edges of the fields, eyes on the ground. Found two more stones, smaller than the first. Pocketed them both. At midday meal, he asked the woman who ladled the quinoa if anyone in the village worked metal.

"The priest has a silver cup. Came from Quito."

"I meant for trade. Smelting, refining."

She looked at him for a long moment. "Why would we?"

He couldn't think of an answer that wouldn't sound stupid, so he said nothing.

A month passed. He worked his two days, ate from the common pot, slept in the guest house. The council met twice more—once about a broken water gate, once about whether to send extra wool down to Quito to trade for more iron tools before winter. Both times they asked if he had opinions. Both times he said no, because he didn't understand enough about their water or their wool or their winter to have any.

In his pocket, the stones multiplied. He walked the stream banks at dawn, turned over rocks after rain, pried them from crevices in the low walls that bordered the upper terraces. Nobody stopped him. Nobody seemed to care.

In the second month, he went down to Quito with the monthly trade party. Six young men, twelve llamas, bundles of dyed wool and carved bowls packed in woven bags. They left before dawn and walked in silence, the path steep and narrow enough that conversation would have been wasted breath.

Quito was noise and stink and crowds. Diego had forgotten how cities smelled—shit and cooking oil and too many people pressed together. The market square was full of merchants arguing in three languages, weighing goods on scales that probably hadn't been calibrated in years.

One of the young men from the basin knew where to go. They unloaded the wool and bowls at a stall run by a thin man with ink-stained fingers who counted everything twice and paid in a mixture of coin and promissory notes. The young men divided the coin, bought salt and iron tools and paper, and started the walk back.

Diego stayed behind.

He found a merchant who dealt in metals—a Portuguese man with a scale that looked more honest than most. Showed him the three largest stones.

The merchant's eyes changed. Not much. Just enough.

"Where did you get these?"

"Found them."

"Where?"

Diego said nothing.

The merchant weighed them, scratched one with a knife, held it up to the light. "I can give you forty pesos."

"Eighty."

"Fifty. And that's generous."

Diego took the fifty. It was more money than he'd held in six months.

He bought a good coat, a new pair of boots, and a leather bag. Spent two nights in a boarding house with a real bed and a door that locked. Ate meat twice a day. Drank wine that didn't taste like vinegar.

On the third day, he started asking about ships.

The basin looked smaller when he got back. Or maybe he looked at it differently. The terraces still stepped down in their careful geometry, the canal still split the water into equal shares, the council still met under the pepper tree. But now he saw it as a kind of prison—beautiful, well-ordered, and completely closed.

The old man asked him how the city was.

"Loud," Diego said.

"Always is. That's why we live up here."

Diego wanted to say: You live up here because you don't know what you have. Because you use gold for doorstops and grow quinoa when you could be buying whatever you want. Because you think sharing a pot of stew is prosperity when there are people down there who eat off silver plates.

He said nothing.

In the third month, he started collecting systematically. A stone a day, sometimes two. He learned which parts of the stream yielded more, which rocks to turn over, which crevices to check. The young woman who worked beside him in the fields noticed once, watched him pocket a stone, and went back to weeding without comment.

The weight in his bag grew heavier.

At night he lay awake and calculated. Three months of collection. Maybe four. Enough to book passage to Lima, maybe beyond. Enough to start again, properly this time, with capital and connections and the kind of cushion that kept you from having to run in the middle of the night.

The council met to discuss the guest house roof. The thatch was rotting in one corner, and rain was getting in. They debated whether to replace just the damaged section or redo the whole thing. Someone suggested that since Diego was still staying there, maybe he could help with the work.

He said he would.

He didn't.

The fourth month, he went down to Quito again. This time he carried a bag heavy enough to make the llamas look at him with something like judgment. The same merchant weighed the stones, scratched them, offered him a hundred and twenty pesos.

Diego took it.

He bought passage on a mule train heading for the coast. Two weeks to Guayaquil, the muleteer said. From there, ships went everywhere.

When he got back to the basin, he told the old man he was leaving.

"Safe travels," the old man said.

"That's it? Safe travels?"

"What else?"

Diego had expected something. Argument, maybe. Questions. Some acknowledgment that he was leaving with enough gold to buy a house, that he'd spent four months picking their mountains clean while they fed him and asked nothing in return.

The old man smiled slightly. "The path is yours to walk."

Diego left at dawn. Alone this time, no llamas, just his bag and his coat and his new boots. The stones pressed against his back with each step. At the shrine, he didn't leave a candle.

The mule train was waiting in the valley below. Diego paid half the passage up front, the rest on arrival. The muleteer counted the coins twice and gestured to a spot in the line.

"Where you coming from?" another traveler asked.

"Up there." Diego pointed at the mountains.

"Anything worth seeing?"

Diego thought about the terraced fields, the canal with its carved gates, the council under the pepper tree. About the woman who laughed at his silver real. About the common pot and the shared labor and the people who used gold for doorstops because they couldn't imagine what else it might be worth.

"No," he said. "Nothing worth seeing."

The mule train moved slowly. They made camp each night, the muleteer charging extra for food that wasn't as good as the basin's communal quinoa. Other travelers talked about opportunities in Guayaquil, about ships and trade routes and fortunes to be made. Diego listened and said little.

On the third day, a man on horseback caught up with the train. Well-dressed, good leather, a sword that looked like it had been used. He rode up and down the line, looking at faces, and stopped beside Diego.

"You came from the mountain basin?"

"Yes."

"Carrying anything interesting?"

Diego's hand went to his bag. "Just personal effects."

The man smiled. It wasn't a friendly smile. "I represent certain interests in Quito. We've been hearing about someone removing valuable materials from that area. Materials that might properly belong to the Crown, or at least to parties with legitimate claims."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Let me be clearer." The man leaned down from his horse. "You're carrying gold you didn't mine, from land you don't own, in a region where mineral rights are complicated. I'm offering to simplify things. You give me half of what you're carrying, and I forget I saw you."

Diego looked at the muleteer. The muleteer was suddenly very interested in adjusting his mule's pack.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we'll discuss it with the magistrate in Guayaquil. Which will take time. And money. And might end with you in a cell while lawyers argue about jurisdiction."

Diego gave him half.

The man weighed the stones, pocketed them, and rode off without another word.

In Guayaquil, Diego found a merchant who'd buy the rest. This one offered less—the gold wasn't as pure as the first batch, he said, though Diego suspected the real issue was that he looked desperate. Diego took what he could get.

After the merchant's cut, the muleteer's final payment, lodging, and food, he had enough for passage on a coastal trader heading south. Not to Lima. Not to anywhere with real opportunities. Just down the coast to another port where the same conversations would happen with different faces and the money would run out a little more each time.

He stood on the dock and watched ships being loaded. Somewhere up in the mountains, the basin's council was probably meeting under the pepper tree. Someone was probably ladling quinoa from the common pot. The canal gates were probably opening and closing with the same creak they'd had for generations.

Diego thought about the old man's smile. About the woman who'd pushed his silver real back into his palm. About the young woman in the fields who'd watched him pocket stones and said nothing.

They'd known. Of course they'd known. They'd watched him collect their "doorstops" for four months and never said a word because what he was doing—walking away from coordinated abundance toward extractive scarcity—probably seemed as incomprehensible to them as their refusal to monetize gold seemed to him.

The ship's captain was calling for final boarding.

Diego picked up his bag—lighter now, much lighter—and walked up the gangplank. The coast waited. And after the coast, other ports. Other opportunities. Other merchants with scales and hard eyes and offers that would sound generous until you counted what you'd started with.

Behind him, the mountains were already disappearing into evening haze. The path back up existed. The shrine was still there. The basin would still be there, terraces and canal and common pot and all.

Diego had enough coin for passage.

He didn't have enough to go back.

The ship pulled away from the dock, and he stood at the rail watching Guayaquil recede. In his pocket, his fingers found one last stone—small, not worth carrying down to sell. He turned it over in his hand, felt its weight, and finally understood what the woman with the broom had meant.

Keep your little moon. You'll need it when you leave.

He dropped the stone into the water and watched it sink.
