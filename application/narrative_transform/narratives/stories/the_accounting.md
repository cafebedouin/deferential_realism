# The Accounting

The forms arrived on a Tuesday. Medical certification, the letter said. Required for continued enrollment in the clinical study. Dr. Reeves needed them by Friday or they'd have to withdraw her from the protocol.

Sarah read it twice at the kitchen table, the paper shaking slightly in her hands. The trial was paying for Mom's cancer treatment—the expensive kind, the kind their insurance had already rejected twice. Without it, they were looking at sixty thousand dollars out of pocket for the next six months. Money they didn't have.

"It's just paperwork," her mother said from the living room, not looking up from her book. "Your father can handle it."

Her father had been dead for three years.

Sarah didn't correct her. The medication did this sometimes—made the past feel present, made gone people seem temporarily available. Dr. Reeves said it was a known side effect. Acceptable, given the alternative.

She called the study coordinator. "My mother can't really travel right now. Can we do the certification remotely?"

"I'm afraid not. The protocol requires in-person assessment at the Baltimore facility. It's FDA mandated for this phase of the trial." The coordinator's voice was pleasant, apologetic. "We can schedule transport if cost is a concern."

"It's not the cost." Sarah looked at her mother, who'd gone back to reading. They'd gotten her comfortable in that chair over months of adjustment. The right cushions. The angle that didn't aggravate her back. The proximity to the bathroom. "She's stable here. Moving her—"

"I understand. But without the certification, we can't continue treatment. Insurance liability."

After she hung up, Sarah did the math. Three hours to Baltimore in good traffic. Six hours round trip, minimum. Her mother couldn't sit upright in a car for more than twenty minutes anymore. They'd need to stop. Frequently. And the assessment itself would take how long? The coordinator had said "approximately ninety minutes."

Her mother's oncologist had been clear: missing even one treatment dose would compromise the entire protocol sequence. The drugs built on each other. Skip a week, start over. If you could start over. The trial only accepted patients once.

Sarah looked at the calendar. The next scheduled treatment was Thursday. The Baltimore appointment could only be Wednesday or Friday, the coordinator had said. Wednesday meant pulling her mother out before this week's treatment. Friday meant hoping nothing happened in the gap.

Wednesday or Friday. But not Thursday. Never Thursday, which was when it would have worked.

She thought about calling her brother. Michael was a lawyer. He understood contracts, requirements, institutional logic. But Michael was in Seattle managing a case that, he'd explained carefully when he couldn't come for Mom's birthday, would determine his partnership track. "I'll visit at Christmas," he'd promised. That was four months away.

Their father would have driven to Baltimore and argued with someone until they made an exception. Their father had believed exceptions were always possible if you knew the right person to ask. That's what killed him, actually—believing the chest pain was exceptional, that his case was different, that he could wait until after the meeting to go to the hospital.

Sarah filled out the first three pages of the medical certification form. Name, date of birth, patient ID number. Then she hit the section about current medications.

Her mother was on seven different drugs. The study medication. Two for pain management. Something for nausea. Something for her thyroid. Something for anxiety. Something for sleep. The form wanted dosages, prescribing physicians, dates of initiation, documented side effects, interactions.

She didn't know half of this. It was in her mother's pill organizer, maybe. Written on bottles in the bathroom cabinet. Some of it was probably in whatever patient portal her mother's primary care doctor used, if Sarah could remember the login credentials.

The form asked who managed the patient's medication schedule. Sarah wrote her own name, then stopped. The question below asked about the manager's medical qualifications. She left it blank.

Her mother called from the living room. "Sarah? Do we have any of those crackers left? The ones your father likes?"

"I'll look," Sarah said.

She knew they didn't. She'd stopped buying them after the funeral. But she went to the pantry anyway and moved things around, making searching noises, giving her mother time to forget the question.

When she came back to the table, she read the form's fine print. Incomplete submissions would delay processing. Delayed processing might result in interrupted treatment coverage. Interrupted coverage would terminate study enrollment.

The certification was due Friday. It was Tuesday afternoon. She was supposed to be at work. She'd already used most of her sick days for her mother's appointments, and her manager had started asking questions about "sustainable coverage plans."

Sarah picked up her phone and called the Baltimore facility directly. Maybe if she explained—

An automated system answered. Press 1 for appointments. Press 2 for billing. Press 3 for clinical trials. She pressed 3. Another menu. Press 1 for enrollment questions. Press 2 for current participants. Press 3 for physicians. She pressed 2.

"All study coordinators are currently assisting other participants. Your estimated wait time is forty-seven minutes. Please stay on the line."

She stayed on the line. She opened her laptop and searched for her mother's patient portal login. She found three different medical systems her mother might be registered in. She tried the passwords she knew her mother used. None of them worked.

The hold music was baroque. Something with violins.

Her mother appeared in the doorway. "Are you coming to bed?"

"It's three in the afternoon, Mom."

"Oh." Her mother looked confused, then embarrassed. "I thought it was later."

"Do you want to lie down?"

"No, I want—" Her mother stopped, frowned. "I wanted something. I came in here for something."

"Take your time."

Her mother stood there, holding the doorframe, trying to remember. Sarah wanted to help but didn't know what the question was. After a moment, her mother shuffled back toward the living room.

The hold music played. Sarah found a bottle of her mother's thyroid medication and copied the information onto the form. Dosage: 50mcg. Prescribing physician: Dr. K. Ramos. Except she wasn't sure if it was K or H. The handwriting on the label was unclear.

She checked the clock. Twenty-three minutes on hold.

The form asked about family medical history. Father's cause of death. Sarah wrote "myocardial infarction" and then wondered if they wanted the specific type. The death certificate was somewhere in her mother's filing cabinet, but looking for it felt like excavating a grave.

Mother's parents: both deceased, causes unknown. Her mother had never talked about them much. Some kind of falling out before Sarah was born.

Siblings: her mother had a sister in Phoenix they hadn't spoken to in fifteen years. Sarah didn't know why. She'd asked once. Her mother had said, "It's complicated," in a tone that ended the conversation.

The form wanted contact information for next of kin. Sarah wrote Michael's name and Seattle address. Then she stared at it. If something happened, would they really call Michael first? Would he come?

"Your estimated wait time is thirty-one minutes."

Sarah hung up. She'd call back later. Or maybe she'd just drive to Baltimore and camp in the waiting room until someone talked to her.

She returned to the medication list. The pain medication—she knew that one. Prescribed by the oncologist. But the starting date? Sometime after the diagnosis. February, maybe. Or March. The diagnosis had been February 3rd, she remembered that exactly, but the prescriptions had come later, in a blur of appointments and pharmacy visits.

She wrote "Feb 2023" and moved on.

Her mother called again. "Sarah?"

"Yes?"

"Did your father already leave for work?"

Sarah closed her eyes. "Yes, Mom. He left early."

"All right. I just wanted to know."

The lie came easier than the truth. The truth would upset her mother, who would then forget being upset but remain agitated for hours. Sarah had learned this. You couldn't correct the delusion. You could only redirect it.

She wondered if that counted as managing her mother's care. If that qualified her for the form's questions about caregiver competence.

The medication for anxiety—that was new, prescribed by someone her mother had seen once when the regular oncologist was on vacation. Sarah couldn't remember that doctor's name. It might be in the discharge paperwork from that appointment, which might be in the folder she'd started keeping, which was somewhere in the house.

She left that line blank.

The form asked about transportation needs. Would the patient require assistance during transit? Sarah checked yes. Would the patient require medical supervision during transit? She didn't know. Probably not? But if something happened in the car, if her mother got disoriented or if the pain spiked—

She checked yes. Then she saw the note below: "Patients requiring medical supervision must be accompanied by a licensed healthcare provider or certified medical transport service."

Certified medical transport would cost what? Three hundred dollars? More? And her mother would be alone in an ambulance with strangers, which would frighten her, which would make everything harder.

Sarah crossed out the yes and checked no instead.

The form continued. Three more pages of questions about mobility, cognitive function, activities of daily living. Could the patient dress herself? Usually. Feed herself? Yes. Manage her own medication? No. Recognize familiar faces? Sometimes.

Sarah filled it in, making small choices that she knew weren't small, that would determine things she couldn't predict.

When she finished, it was dark outside. She hadn't noticed the afternoon leaving. Her mother was asleep in the living room chair, the book still open in her lap.

Sarah folded the forms and put them in her bag. She'd mail them tomorrow. Maybe they'd be enough. Maybe the study would accept incomplete information. Maybe there was flexibility in the system she just hadn't found yet.

Or maybe they'd call on Monday and say the forms were insufficient, that her mother was being dropped from the trial, that they should contact their insurance company about next steps.

She sat at the table and didn't move. Through the doorway, she could see her mother's chest rising and falling. Steady. Regular. Still here.

For now, that was enough. For now, she didn't have to know what came next.

The forms could wait until morning. The drive to Baltimore could wait. The impossible calculations of what was required versus what was possible could wait.

Sarah closed her laptop and sat in the quiet house, listening to her mother breathe, not planning, not solving, just existing in the narrow space between one demand and the next, where nothing had collapsed yet but nothing was resolved.

She would call the coordinator again tomorrow. She would find the right person to ask. She would make it work somehow.

She had to.

But tonight, she just sat there, holding the folded forms, while her mother slept and the clock moved forward and the Friday deadline approached like weather—inevitable, indifferent, already in motion.
