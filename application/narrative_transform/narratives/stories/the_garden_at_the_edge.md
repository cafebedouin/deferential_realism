# The Garden at the Edge

The tutor's room smelled of lamp oil and mildew. Tomás had stopped opening the shutters after the third time someone from the village threw stones at them—not from malice, he thought, but because the window was there and stones were plentiful and there was little else to do after the vines were pruned. He sat at the scarred table with his Leibniz, the pages soft as cloth from handling, and read again about sufficient reason, about the pre-established harmony, about the world as it must be.

Down in the kitchen, the cook was explaining to her daughter why they couldn't leave. "Where would we go? Your father's father worked this land. His name is in the church book. The baron's seal is on everything—the bridge, the mill, the weights at market. You think you can just walk away from a seal?"

Tomás had tried explaining it differently to the baron's son that morning. "Consider," he'd said, "that every arrangement serves a purpose. The vines grow on this hillside because the soil drains properly. The village clusters around the church because water flows there. Each thing in its place, each place necessary."

The boy—António, fifteen and soft-handed—had looked at him with the blankness of someone who'd never questioned why anything was where it was. "But why did Rodrigo get beaten yesterday?"

"The steward said he was late to the pressing."

"But he wasn't late. I saw him. He was early."

Tomás had felt something shift in his chest, a small displacement, like a stone settling deeper into mud. "Perhaps the steward saw differently."

"The steward's always seeing differently." António picked at the embroidery on his sleeve—the family's coat of arms reduced to decoration, four quarterings his mother had spent six months verifying with a monastery in Évora. "My father says Rodrigo's family owes three years of rent. Says the beating was a reminder."

The room had been very quiet then. Through the window, the terraced vines stepped down toward the village, each row held by a stone wall built by hands that were dust now, maintained by hands that would be dust soon. Tomás had opened his mouth to explain about divine order, about how debt creates obligation and obligation creates structure, and how structure allows society to function. The words had been there, ready, the same words he'd written in his dissertation at Coimbra.

He'd closed his mouth. António had noticed.

"You don't believe it," the boy said.

"I believe," Tomás said carefully, "that this is how things work."

That had been three months ago. Now it was October, and the baron had called Tomás to his study—a room that smelled of gout and old paper and the specific sourness of bodies that no longer moved much. The baron sat with his leg propped up, the foot swollen purple inside its slipper.

"I'm sending António to Lisbon," the baron said without preamble. "To my cousin's household. He needs to learn how things are done at court."

Tomás nodded. It was the progression he'd expected—village to provincial estate to capital to colonial appointment, each step requiring the right papers, the right blood, the right connections.

"You'll go with him. As his companion. My cousin will pay your keep."

Something in Tomás went very still. "I'm honored, senhor."

"Don't be. You're a convenience." The baron shifted, winced. "António asks too many questions. You'll teach him which ones to stop asking."

After Tomás left, he walked down to the village. Not to anywhere particular. Just down. The path was rutted from the last rain, and his shoes—the good ones, saved for teaching—sank into the mud. He passed the church where the new tax had been posted, written in language nobody in the village could read except the priest, who read it slowly and with many pauses, as if he couldn't quite believe it himself.

The cooper was outside his shop, hands wrapped around a cup of something. He nodded to Tomás. They'd never spoken, but there was a recognition between them—both men who worked with their hands on other people's property, both men whose wages came late, both men whose names were written in ledgers they never saw.

"Hear you're going to the capital," the cooper said.

"The baron told you?"

"The baron tells his steward. The steward tells his wife. His wife tells the priest. The priest tells everyone." The cooper drank. "You won't come back."

"The baron said it was temporary."

"What the baron says and what happens aren't the same thing." The cooper looked at his hands, then at Tomás. "My father made barrels for the old baron. I make barrels for this one. My son will make barrels for the next. That's temporary too, if you think about it right. It just lasts longer than a life."

Tomás walked back up the hill as the sun was setting. The shadows of the cypress trees stretched long across the terraces, and from this angle you couldn't see the village at all, just the vines and the church tower and the hills rolling away toward the sea. It looked, he thought, exactly like the engravings in his philosophy books—the orderly world, everything in proportion.

He'd believed it once. Not just recited it for examinations, but believed it in his chest, in his sleep, in the way he walked through the world. Every particular arranged for universal good. Every suffering a necessary part of harmony. Every constraint proof of design.

The belief had left so quietly he couldn't say when. There'd been no single moment. Just a long series of small observations that accumulated like silt. The way the baron's seal worked. The way debt compounded. The way the steward's cane fell on the same backs, month after month, while the account books showed the same debts growing larger. The way António would inherit this, and the villagers would inherit nothing, and everyone called it Providence.

In his room, he lit the lamp and opened the Leibniz again. The words were still there, still logically sound, still proving that this world was the best of all possible worlds because God had chosen it. He could follow the argument. Could teach it. Could defend it against objections.

He just couldn't make it mean what it had meant.

There was a knock. The baron's daughter—Isabel, seventeen and sharper than her brother—stood in the doorway. She had her father's account books under her arm.

"My father said you're leaving."

"If God wills it."

She made a small sound, not quite a laugh. "God wills everything here. It's very convenient." She set the books on his table. "I wanted to show you something. The entries for the village."

Tomás didn't open them. "I don't need to see the accounts."

"I think you do. Look at the Rodrigo family. Three years of debt, my father said."

"I heard him."

"Look at the dates." She flipped open the middle book, pointed. "The original debt was from buying seed after the hail storm. Twenty-three réis. See the interest rate? Twelve percent yearly. See the penalties for late payment? Five réis per month. See the cost of flour from our mill, charged against the debt? See the rental on his cottage, also charged?"

Tomás looked. The numbers were very clear.

"The debt grows faster than he can possibly pay it," Isabel said. "It's designed to. It's been designed to since before my grandfather's time. The whole village is designed to." She closed the book. "You teach António about sufficient reason. Here's sufficient reason. We need them to stay."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because you're leaving. Because once you're in Lisbon, António will learn how this works from my cousin, who learned it from his father, who learned it from his. And you won't be able to pretend you taught him philosophy instead." She picked up the books. "My father thinks you'll teach António which questions to stop asking. I think António will teach you which answers to stop giving."

After she left, Tomás sat in the dark. The lamp had burned low. Outside, he could hear singing from the village—one of the feast days, probably, though he'd lost track. The singing was in Portuguese, but old Portuguese, the words worn smooth by repetition until they meant less than the melody.

He thought about Lisbon. About the baron's cousin's household, which would be larger than this one, more complex, connected to more villages, more debt, more account books. He thought about António learning to read those books the way Isabel had learned to read them—not as moral failures but as mechanisms. Useful mechanisms.

He thought about the cooper's son, making barrels.

Tomás pulled out paper and began writing a letter to the prior at Coimbra, asking if there might be a position in the monastery library. Copywork, perhaps. Catalog maintenance. Something quiet. The monastery had gardens, he remembered. Small ones, enclosed by walls, where the brothers grew herbs for medicine.

He would probably not send the letter. The baron had already told his cousin to expect them. The seal had already been affixed to whatever documents mattered. And there was a kind of peace in understanding that—in seeing exactly how large the machine was, exactly how small his position in it, exactly how much his philosophy had ever mattered.

The singing from the village had stopped. Tomás blew out the lamp and sat in the darkness, listening to the wind in the cypress trees, and did not think about sufficient reason or pre-established harmony or the best of all possible worlds.

He thought about gardens. Small ones. Enclosed. Where you could see the walls clearly and know exactly how far you could walk, and never have to pretend the walls were something else.
