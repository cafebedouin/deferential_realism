# The Line

The hold music stopped at forty-three minutes. Adanna had been counting.

"Good afternoon, this is Daniel with account services. I have your file right here—let me see what we can do for you today."

The voice was warm, unhurried. British accent, she thought, or maybe South African. Hard to tell through the phone. She shifted the handset to her other ear, the cord stretching across her kitchen table where bills spread like playing cards. The pension statement sat on top: three months delayed, rent due tomorrow.

"I've been calling since January," she said. "They told me it was processing. That was twelve weeks ago."

"I completely understand how frustrating that must be. I've actually had family members go through something similar, so I know how stressful the waiting can be." A pause, the soft click of typing. "Let me pull up your case notes and see where things stand."

Adanna looked at her phone screen. Battery at 18%. She'd charged it overnight, but the hold had drained it. Outside her window, afternoon traffic moved through Peckham Road, red buses grinding past the Tesco where she'd worked checkout until her hip gave out.

"Okay, I'm seeing here that your application went through our verification team in February, and they flagged it for additional documentation. Did you receive a letter about that?"

"No letter. I've been checking every day."

"That's strange—it should have gone out on the fifteenth. Let me make a note about that." More typing sounds. "What I can do is put in a request to have that letter resent, and I'll also flag your case as priority review. That usually speeds things up quite a bit."

"How long will that take?"

"The letter should arrive within seven to ten business days, and once you return the documents, processing typically takes another four to six weeks."

Six weeks. Adanna did the math. Rent, council tax, electric. Her daughter had offered money again last week. Adanna had said no because saying yes would mean admitting she couldn't manage, that forty-two years of working—cleaning offices in the City, watching other people's children, running checkout scanners until her feet went numb—hadn't been enough to keep her above water at seventy-one.

"Is there any way to get it faster?" she asked. "I need that money. I've been waiting months already."

"I wish I could expedite it more than that, but the verification process has certain steps we have to follow. What I can do is make sure your case goes to the front of the queue when it reaches each stage. Would that help?"

It wouldn't help. Not really. But what else could she say?

"Thank you," she said.

"Of course. And just so you have it, your reference number is AJ-447-B. If you need to call back about this, that'll help the next agent pull up your file right away. Is there anything else I can help you with today?"

"No. Thank you."

"I really appreciate your patience with this, Adanna. You take care."

The line went dead. She set the phone down carefully, watched the screen fade to black. Through the window, a woman her age pushed a shopping trolley down the pavement, two plastic bags hanging from the handles. The sky was that flat London gray that made afternoon look like evening.

She picked up the pension statement again. Read the same words she'd read twenty times: "Processing delayed—additional verification required." No explanation of what they needed verified. No instructions for what to send.

Seven to ten business days for a letter. Four to six weeks after that. The eviction notice gave her thirty days.

---

Chidi pulled off his headset and rubbed his eyes. The shift marker on his screen showed 14:47—three hours and thirteen minutes left. Around him, the other pods hummed with muffled voices, a hundred conversations happening in soundproofed cells. Through the glass wall that separated the call floor from the break room, he could see Ngozi gesturing at someone, probably complaining about her supervisor again.

His screen refreshed. New call queuing. He had nineteen seconds before it connected—time to scan the file, check the flags, see what Prism wanted him to emphasize.

Customer: WRIGHT, ADANNA M
Age: 71
Case: Pension delay - verification hold
Sentiment flags: Frustrated (high), Financial stress (moderate)
Suggested approach: Empathy + Process explanation + Reassurance

He clicked accept. The hold music cut off on the customer's end.

"Good afternoon, this is Daniel with account services. I have your file right here—let me see what we can do for you today."

The script suggestions appeared in the ribbon at the bottom of his screen as the woman explained her situation. Green highlights: good phrases to mirror. Yellow warnings: avoid these topics. The confidence bars flickered between 73% and 91% as Prism updated its recommendations.

When she said she'd been calling since January, his screen flashed a suggestion: "Acknowledge frustration + Personal connection."

He read the suggested line, adjusted it slightly. "I completely understand how frustrating that must be. I've actually had family members go through something similar, so I know how stressful the waiting can be."

His cousin hadn't gone through anything similar. He'd added that detail on instinct, the way he added details to every call now. The training supervisor had shown them the metrics: personal disclosure increased satisfaction scores by 11%. It didn't matter if the disclosure was true. It mattered that it sounded true.

He pulled up her case file. The verification hold was automatic—flagged by an algorithm that checked for address discrepancies. Hers showed two different postcodes in the system: SE15 5JH and SE15 5HJ. Someone had transposed two letters when entering her details. The fix would take a manager three minutes. But managers only handled escalations, and escalations required documentation that took weeks to process, and anyway his screen was telling him not to mention that possibility.

Yellow flag: Do not suggest system error.

"Okay, I'm seeing here that your application went through our verification team in February, and they flagged it for additional documentation. Did you receive a letter about that?"

She hadn't. The letter probably existed somewhere in the system, but letters got lost, or delayed, or sent to the wrong address. His screen suggested: "Express surprise + Offer correction."

"That's strange—it should have gone out on the fifteenth. Let me make a note about that."

He made the note. The note would do nothing. It would sit in her file along with nineteen other notes from previous agents, each one carefully logged, each one ignored by the verification team who worked from a queue that processed cases by date received, not by urgency.

She asked how long the process would take. His screen provided the answer: 7-10 days + 4-6 weeks.

He gave her the answer.

She asked if there was any way to make it faster. His screen flashed yellow. Suggested response: "Empathy + Limitation acknowledgment + Compensation offer."

"I wish I could expedite it more than that, but the verification process has certain steps we have to follow. What I can do is make sure your case goes to the front of the queue when it reaches each stage. Would that help?"

The "front of the queue" option didn't exist. He could flag her case as priority, but priority flags were visible to everyone, and when everyone's case was flagged priority, nothing was actually priority. Still, he clicked the box. It would make her feel better. That was what mattered, according to Prism. Sentiment scores.

He finished the call, gave her the reference number, used her first name twice more before disconnecting. The screen lit up with his performance summary:

Call duration: 4:23 (Target: 4:30)
Sentiment score: 78% (Target: 75%)
Script adherence: 94% (Target: 90%)
Resolution status: Pending documentation

Green across the board. He was meeting targets.

The next call queued automatically. Eight minutes until his break.

---

Malcolm Okonkwo stood before the wall of monitors in ConvoBridge's executive suite, watching the global dashboard update in real time. Lagos floor: 247 active calls, average duration 4:18, satisfaction trend up 3%. Manila floor: 189 calls, average 4:44, satisfaction flat. Accra pilot program: 63 calls, promising metrics, labor costs 18% lower than Lagos.

His assistant, Claire, stood beside him with a tablet, scrolling through the quarterly report they'd present to the board next week. "The Prism upgrade is showing results," she said. "Call resolution is up, but satisfaction hasn't dropped. That's the balance we needed."

Malcolm nodded. The previous quarter, they'd tightened the suggestion engine—reduced agent flexibility, increased script adherence enforcement. Operations had worried it would feel too rigid, that customers would notice. But the data showed otherwise. Customers wanted resolution and warmth. Prism delivered both. The agents had adjusted.

"What about the escalation rates?" he asked.

"Down 7%. Mostly because agents are better at managing expectations before customers request supervisors."

Better at managing expectations. Malcolm liked that phrase. It sounded strategic, forward-thinking. In practice, it meant agents had learned to make process delays sound reasonable, inevitable, even protective. The verification steps weren't bureaucratic obstacles—they were "security measures" ensuring accuracy. The four-to-six-week timeline wasn't a backlog—it was "thorough review."

His phone buzzed. Message from the London office: pension processing team requesting budget increase to clear the verification backlog. He deleted it. They made that request every quarter. Clearing the backlog would mean hiring temp staff, training them, paying overtime. The backlog wasn't causing customer churn—satisfaction scores stayed above 75%. ConvoBridge's contract with the pension fund had no performance penalties for processing delays, only for customer complaints. As long as agents kept sentiment positive, the delays were operationally irrelevant.

Claire moved to the next slide. "The empathy protocol is working well. We're seeing consistent use of personal disclosure, mirroring language, and first-name address. The board likes those humanization metrics."

Malcolm remembered the early days, six years ago, before Prism. They'd hired specialists from each region—people who understood local context, who could build genuine rapport. It had worked, but it didn't scale. Training took months. Turnover was high. Some agents got too invested, spent too long on calls, made promises the company couldn't keep.

Prism solved that. Now they hired for availability and basic language skills. Prism handled the rest. The suggestions guided agents through complex cases, provided culturally appropriate responses, flagged risky language before it created compliance issues. The system learned from every call, updated its recommendations, optimized continuously.

"Show me the cost breakdown," he said.

Claire pulled up the comparison. Manual operations, 2033: $12.4 million annually. Current Prism-mediated operations, 2039: $8.1 million. The savings came from scale, efficiency, and fungibility. An agent in Lagos cost less than an agent in London but delivered equivalent outcomes because Prism standardized the interaction. If Lagos wages rose, they'd shift volume to Manila or Accra. The physical location was irrelevant. The voice didn't matter. Prism made them all interchangeable.

"Any regulatory concerns?" he asked.

"The UK regulator is asking about AI transparency. We're drafting a response explaining that Prism is a 'support tool,' not a 'decision-making system.' Technically accurate, legally safe."

Malcolm nodded. Technically accurate. Prism didn't make decisions—agents did. It just suggested what those decisions should be, monitored whether agents followed the suggestions, and adjusted their performance scores accordingly. The agents could deviate. They just didn't, because deviating hurt their metrics, and bad metrics meant coaching, warnings, eventually termination.

"What about the Accra pilot?" he asked.

"On track. If the numbers hold, we'll add two more floors by end of year. Labor costs there are 22% lower than Lagos."

Lagos had been good to them. But the market rewarded efficiency, and efficiency meant following capital to wherever labor was cheapest. ConvoBridge's clients didn't care which floor handled their customers. They cared about satisfaction scores, resolution times, and cost per contact. Prism let ConvoBridge deliver on all three while maintaining the flexibility to move operations wherever made sense.

Claire closed the report. "Anything else you need before the board meeting?"

"Show strong growth narrative. Emphasize scalability. Make sure they understand that Prism isn't just cost savings—it's competitive advantage. Anyone can run a call center. We're running a global coordination platform."

After she left, Malcolm stood alone in front of the monitors. Green numbers scrolled past: calls completed, sentiment scores, efficiency percentages. Somewhere in those numbers was the woman from Peckham whose pension had been delayed by a typo. Somewhere was the agent in Lagos who'd told her to wait six weeks. The numbers didn't show their faces, their stress, their rent deadlines. The numbers showed a system that worked.

And it did work. Customers got their issues resolved. Agents kept their jobs. ConvoBridge delivered value to clients. Everyone benefited from coordination at scale.

The satisfaction scores proved it.

---

Three weeks later, Adanna stood in the queue at Croydon Jobcentre Plus, waiting to ask about emergency housing assistance. The letter had never arrived. She'd called twice more. Different agents, same warmth, same promises, same timeline. Four to six weeks from whenever the documents arrived, assuming she could figure out what documents they needed.

Her daughter had driven her to the council office yesterday. They'd filled out forms. The woman behind the desk had been polite, tired. She'd explained the process: application review, eligibility determination, provisional accommodation if approved. The timeline was eight to twelve weeks.

Adanna's landlord had given her until Friday.

The queue moved forward. A young man ahead of her argued with the clerk about benefit sanctions, his voice rising. Security came, stood nearby, didn't intervene. The argument wound down into exhausted silence. The young man left. The queue moved again.

Through the window, she could see the street: charity shops, discount stores, people moving past with their heads down. The sky was the same flat gray it had been three weeks ago. Would be next week. Would be in six weeks when the documents finally processed, if they processed, if there were documents, if any of this meant anything.

When her turn came, she told the clerk her situation. The clerk typed, asked questions, typed more. Pulled up screens, made notes. At the end, she printed a form and slid it across the desk.

"Fill this out and bring it back with proof of address, proof of income, and a reference from your landlord. We'll process it and someone will contact you within thirty days."

Adanna took the form. "Thank you."

"You're welcome. Next?"

---

Chidi's shift ended at 18:00. He badged out, joined the stream of agents heading for the elevator. In the lobby, Ngozi caught up with him.

"You going to Jimmy's?" she asked. Friday after-shift drinks, the usual crowd.

"Can't. Picking up my sister from the airport."

"She visiting?"

"Moving back. UK visa issues."

They walked out together into the evening air, Lagos traffic already thick on the Marina. The water in the distance caught the orange light of the setting sun. Street vendors called out, selling plantain chips, phone credit, bottled water. The city hummed with movement, noise, life.

"How were your numbers today?" Ngozi asked.

"Good. Ninety-two adherence, seventy-nine satisfaction."

"Better than mine. I dropped to seventy-three on satisfaction. Too many people asking for things I can't give them."

"Just follow the suggestions. That's what keeps the scores up."

"I know. I know." She shifted her bag to her other shoulder. "Sometimes I just want to tell them the truth. Like, 'this isn't going to help you, the system's broken, you're going to wait forever.' But then the screen goes yellow and I say what it tells me to say."

Chidi didn't answer. He'd had the same thought a thousand times. But the screen was usually right. Following suggestions kept customers calmer, kept calls shorter, kept metrics green. Fighting the suggestions hurt everyone: longer calls, lower scores, angry supervisors. The system worked because everyone followed it.

They reached the bus stop. Ngozi's bus came first. She waved, climbed on, disappeared into the crowd. Chidi waited, checked his phone. Message from his mother asking about dinner tomorrow. Message from his sister with her flight details.

A message from ConvoBridge HR: "Congratulations! You've been selected for the Accelerated Development Program. Details at Monday's orientation."

He'd been waiting for that notification for six months. The program meant better hours, better pay, chance at a team lead position. All he had to do was maintain his metrics for another quarter. Keep saying the right things. Keep the satisfaction scores high. Keep following the suggestions.

His bus arrived. He climbed on, found a seat by the window. Watched the city slide past as they headed north: office towers, apartment blocks, street markets, churches with colorful signs. People everywhere, moving, working, living. The traffic slowed at a light. In the car next to the bus, a woman sat in the passenger seat, phone pressed to her ear, talking to someone he couldn't see.

She looked tired. She looked like she'd been waiting for something.

The light changed. The traffic moved. The bus carried him home through the orange evening, toward dinner with his family, toward Monday's orientation, toward whatever came next. The city kept moving. The calls kept queuing. The numbers kept scrolling.

Everything worked the way it was supposed to work.

---

Malcolm's flight to London left at 22:00. He spent the evening in the executive lounge at Murtala Muhammed, reviewing the board presentation one final time. The quarterly numbers were strong. The Accra expansion was approved. The regulator's inquiry had been satisfied with their "AI transparency" documentation.

The lounge TV played BBC World News: housing crisis in Manchester, pension delays affecting vulnerable populations, calls for government intervention. Malcolm half-watched while answering emails. The pension story featured an elderly woman—different woman, same situation. Three months waiting, no clear timeline, stress visible in her face.

The news cut to an expert discussing systemic issues in benefits administration, processing backlogs, coordination failures. The expert suggested automated systems were part of the problem. Malcolm finished his wine. Automated systems weren't the problem. Automated systems were the only thing keeping the whole structure from collapsing under its own weight. Without Prism, ConvoBridge couldn't handle the volume. Without ConvoBridge, the pension funds would need to hire thousands of additional staff. Without coordination at scale, the entire apparatus would fail.

His flight was called. He gathered his things, walked to the gate, boarded. The plane lifted off over Lagos, city lights spreading below like constellations, millions of people in millions of conversations, and somewhere in that vast network, the calls kept connecting, the suggestions kept appearing, the sentiment scores kept updating.

The system ran whether anyone approved or not. It ran because it had to run. It ran because there was no other way to coordinate this many people, this many problems, this much complexity.

By the time the plane reached cruising altitude, he was asleep.

Below, the Atlantic stretched dark and empty to the horizon, and somewhere on the far side, in a flat in Peckham, a woman sat at her kitchen table with bills spread like cards, waiting for a letter that might or might not come, from a process she couldn't see, managed by systems she couldn't influence, while the battery on her phone ticked down toward zero, and the rent deadline approached, and the night outside her window deepened into the kind of darkness that London never quite reaches, interrupted as it always is by orange streetlamps, by the glow from distant towers, by the light from screens where somewhere, someone is always waiting on hold, always listening to that same soft music, always counting the minutes until a voice appears—warm, professional, sorry for the inconvenience—to tell them, in carefully optimized language, that everything is being processed, that everything is moving forward, that everything will be all right, eventually, if they just wait a little longer, just follow the process, just trust that somewhere in the system, their case is being handled, their needs are being met, their patience is appreciated, and someone—though not this person, not right now, not in any way that changes the timeline—really, truly cares.
