# The Glow

The rain started around nine. Marcus heard it drumming the flat roof three stories up, sluicing through the downspout past his window. Free water, falling on everyone the same. He'd grown up in Phoenix where rain meant something, where his grandmother kept rain barrels despite the HOA letters. Here it just fell, pooled in the gutters, disappeared into sewers going somewhere he'd never thought to ask about.

His one-bedroom faced the alley between Kimbark and Dorchester. Through the window he could see Mrs. Kowalski's porch light across the way, the orange glow of the streetlamp on the corner, someone's television flickering blue in a second-floor window. The city's perpetual hum of light bounced off the low clouds, turning the night sky the color of old copper. He'd tried to show his niece the Big Dipper last summer when she visited. She'd asked where it was. He'd pointed up at the orange blank and realized he didn't know anymore.

The PowerBook hummed on his desk, Safari open to seven tabs. He'd started looking for a quote from Brecht three hours ago. Now he had the quote—it was in the second paragraph of a PDF he'd downloaded—but also sixteen related articles in his Downloads folder, an email argument thread with someone named DenverProfGuy about Marxist theater, and a forum discussion on the decline of engaged drama that had taken him to sites he couldn't quite remember opening.

His coffee had gone cold. The mug ring on the desk had dried brown.

The essay was due Thursday. Introduction, fifteen minutes. First supporting paragraph, forty-five minutes. Then he'd opened Google to check one fact and found himself reading about East German performance traditions, which led to Piscator, which led to someone's blog about political theater in the 1960s, which had a broken link he'd tried to fix by searching for the site's archive, which—

The laptop was useful. It delivered answers. Before, he would have spent a week tracking down that Brecht quote through interlibrary loan. Now: twelve seconds. But the twelve seconds came with neighbors. The quote arrived dragging twenty other quotes, each with its own set of cousins.

He glanced at the clock in the menu bar. 2:17 AM.

Tomorrow he had composition class at nine. Office hours at one. A reading to attend at seven because the department head kept track of who showed up. The essay was still three paragraphs from done.

His eyes burned. The screen's white glow painted his face, the edge of his desk, the spines of books stacked beside the laptop. Outside, the rain kept falling. Inside, the light stayed on.

---

He'd met Merton in the basement hallway the first week after moving in. The older man had been checking his mailbox—actually checking it, hand on the metal door, removing envelopes—while Marcus juggled grocery bags and tried to find his key.

"You're in 2B," Merton had said. Not a question.

"Yeah. Just started adjuncting at the university. Comp classes, some review work."

"Ah." Merton had closed his mailbox with a metallic click. "Good luck with that."

The older man had turned to go, then paused. "You'll want earplugs. Thursday nights, Mrs. Chen upstairs does her mahjong group until midnight. They get loud."

Over the next few months, Marcus learned a few things about Merton: late sixties, former professor of something—comparative lit, maybe—who'd left the department under circumstances no one quite explained. Now he lived in 1A, a basement unit Marcus had glimpsed once through an open door: floor-to-ceiling bookshelves, a desk with a reading lamp, no computer visible.

They'd started having coffee sometimes. Marcus would knock, Merton would already have the pot going, they'd sit at his kitchen table among the stacks of books.

"You look tired," Merton said one Saturday morning in October.

Marcus rubbed his face. "Late night. Working on a piece about groupthink in postwar drama."

"Find what you needed?"

"Sort of. Found a lot of things. Not sure they were all needed."

Merton poured coffee into mismatched mugs. "The laptop?"

"Makes it easier."

"Does it."

Not a question. Marcus wrapped his hands around the warm ceramic. Through Merton's window—ground-level, looking out at the building's foundation—he could see a strip of sidewalk. Someone walked past in the pre-dawn gray. Just feet, moving left to right, gone.

"I remember when the library closed at ten," Merton said. "You did your research, you went home. The books stayed in the building."

"Now the books come home with you."

"Along with everything else." Merton sipped his coffee. "I've been walking at night. You see them in the windows. Everyone bent the same way, faces lit blue-white. Different buildings, different people. Same posture."

"People are just working."

"Are they?" Merton set down his mug. "I had a colleague once. Brilliant woman. Shakespearean. She said the problem with modern life wasn't that we worked too much. It was that we never stopped working, so we never knew if we'd worked enough."

Marcus thought about the seven tabs still open on his laptop upstairs. He'd meant to close them before coming down. Somehow hadn't.

"The rhinos," Merton said.

"What?"

"Ionesco. You know the play."

Marcus did. Rhinoceros. He'd taught it last spring. One person transforms into a rhino, then another, then everyone in town until only one man's left human, watching the stampede.

"They're not trying to be rhinos," Merton said. "That's the thing everyone forgets. They're just being reasonable. Going along. One person starts, it seems to work for them, so the next person tries it. Eventually you're the strange one for not having a horn."

"You think people are turning into rhinos."

"I think people are doing what makes sense to do. Which is worse."

The sky outside was getting lighter. Marcus needed to get back upstairs, check his email, see if the journal had responded about his last submission, maybe do another search for that secondary source he'd remembered at 1 AM.

"The rain doesn't care," Merton said, looking at the window. "It falls on everyone. Free. No one's charging for it yet."

"Yet?"

"Yet." Merton smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. "Give them time. Someone will figure out how."

---

November came in cold. The streetlamps stayed on until almost eight in the morning now, their orange light mixing with the gray dawn. Marcus walked to campus through air that tasted like wet leaves and car exhaust.

His composition students were writing persuasive essays. The topics this semester: war, patriotism, media bias, the role of protest. They'd turned in rough drafts last week. Marcus had spent Sunday afternoon reading them in the fourth-floor lounge of Harper Library, and by the fifth paper, he'd started noticing a pattern.

They all said the same thing.

Not exactly the same—the vocabulary varied, the examples differed—but the structure was identical. Introduction acknowledging complexity. Body paragraphs citing the same three sources. Conclusion calling for dialogue and understanding. He'd checked. Four students had used the same article from the New York Times. Seven had quoted the same passage from a book he'd assigned but hadn't emphasized. It was a fine passage. It was also on the first page of search results if you typed the essay topic plus "scholarly sources."

In the lounge, a PhD student he didn't know was eating lunch while reading email on her PowerBook. The screen's glow reflected in her glasses. Behind her, through the tall windows, the quad spread out in orange and yellow October color. She wasn't looking at it.

By the time he'd finished grading, his own laptop was open. Just to check one thing. Then another. The battery died around seven. He'd packed his charger. The outlet was right there under the table.

At nine, building security came through telling people the library was closing. Marcus had been reading a forum thread about embedded journalists. He couldn't remember how he'd gotten there.

Walking home, he passed a bar on 55th where three people sat in the window booth, all looking at phones. Their beers sat untouched. In the window's reflection, Marcus caught his own face—pale, eyes shadowed—superimposed over the scene.

He thought about Merton's rhinos. About everyone bending the same way.

His phone buzzed. Email notification. He pulled it out while walking, thumbed it open, read the message. Someone from the online forum had responded. He typed back while waiting at the crosswalk, sent it, pocketed the phone. The light changed. He crossed. The phone buzzed again before he reached the other side.

---

The next Saturday, Merton wasn't home. Marcus knocked twice, waited, knocked again. Finally he went back upstairs. Later, around midnight, he heard footsteps on the stairs and looked out his peephole. Merton, in a rain jacket despite the clear night, heading down to the basement.

Sunday morning, Marcus caught him in the mailroom.

"Went walking," Merton said before Marcus could ask. "Down to the Point and back. Watched the water for a while."

"At midnight?"

"Best time. The lake's actually dark there. Can't see the city. Just water and stars."

Marcus tried to remember the last time he'd seen stars. Not the couple of bright dots that punched through the city glow, but real stars. Plural.

"You should try it," Merton said. "Leave the laptop home."

"I've got a deadline."

"You always have a deadline."

That wasn't fair. Marcus's deadlines were real. The journal wanted the essay by the fifteenth. The department review was in February. He needed publications. He needed to show he was engaged, current, part of the conversation.

"I'm just saying," Merton said, "the lake's been there longer than Google. It'll be there after. You might want to remember what that looks like."

But Marcus had papers to grade that afternoon. And the search he'd started Friday night had led him to a promising archive of performance criticism he wanted to explore. And someone on the forum had raised a good counterpoint to his Brecht interpretation that he needed to address.

The laptop was already open when he got back upstairs. He'd left it in sleep mode. It woke the moment he touched the trackpad, tabs exactly where he'd left them, cursor blinking in the half-finished reply field.

He sat down. Outside, the afternoon sun did its best to penetrate the orange haze. His desk lamp was brighter.

---

Spring semester started with a department meeting. Twenty faculty crammed into a seminar room, laptops open, the department head standing at the front by a projection screen.

"We're implementing a new course assessment system," she said. "You'll log in here"—she clicked, the projector showed a portal—"and enter your learning outcomes, assignments, and grade distributions. Due by the end of week three."

Someone raised a hand. "Is this replacing the system we learned last year?"

"That system's being phased out. This one integrates with the university's enrollment platform."

Groans. The department head kept talking. Marcus opened his laptop—everyone was opening their laptops—and navigated to the portal. It wanted him to create an account. He created an account. It wanted him to verify his email. He checked his email. The verification link sent him to another portal that asked for his university credentials. He entered them. The page refreshed. He was back at the login screen.

Around the room, other people were having the same experience. Someone swore under their breath. Someone else said, "Click the green button, not the blue one." The department head was still talking, something about assessment cycles.

Marcus looked around the room. Everyone bent over their screens. Same posture. Same angle of neck. Same glow on their faces.

He thought: rhinos.

Then he thought: No, that's unfair. We're just doing what we're supposed to do. This is work. This is how work works now.

He clicked the green button. The portal let him in.

---

By March, he'd stopped sleeping properly. Not insomnia exactly—he could sleep if he went to bed. He just didn't go to bed. There was always one more thing to check. One more search to run. One more response to write.

The essay on groupthink was done, submitted, rejected. The feedback was helpful. Revise and resubmit. He'd opened a new document to start revisions. That was in January. The document still had three paragraphs.

Meanwhile, the forum threads kept growing. Someone had challenged his reading of Brecht. He'd responded with a careful argument citing four sources. They'd responded with six sources. He'd gone looking for a seventh. The search had led him to an academic blog, which linked to a journal he'd never heard of, which had an article that contradicted his interpretation but maybe could be incorporated as a counterpoint, which would require restructuring the whole essay.

He'd started restructuring at 11 PM on a Tuesday. At 2 AM, he was still working. At 3 AM, his eyes were watering but he was close to something, he could feel it. At 4 AM, he had seven browser windows open and couldn't remember what the original point was supposed to be.

The alarm went off at seven. He taught at nine.

He taught at nine.

In class, he heard himself talking about topic sentences while thinking about the search he'd abandoned at 4 AM. A student asked a question. He answered it. He thought he answered it. The student nodded, wrote something down. Maybe he'd answered it.

After class, he sat in his office and opened his laptop. The tabs were still there, right where he'd left them. Waiting.

His office hours were 1 to 3. One student came by at 1:15. She stayed twenty minutes. Marcus talked to her about her essay while part of his mind was calculating: if he skipped the reading tonight, he could catch up on the forum discussion, finish the portal assessment that was due, and still get five hours of sleep.

Five hours was fine. He'd done five hours before.

The reading was actually important. Visiting poet, supposed to be good. The department head would notice if he didn't show.

He went. He sat in the back. The poet read for forty-five minutes. Marcus heard every word. Afterward, he couldn't remember a single line.

Walking home, he composed a forum post in his head. About embedded journalists. About truth and bias. About something. The streetlamps were already on, even though the sun hadn't fully set. The orange glow painted the sidewalk, the parked cars, the wet pavement where rain had fallen earlier.

Free rain. No one charging. Yet.

At home, he sat at his desk. The laptop opened. The screen glowed white, then blue, then filled with tabs. His email had thirty-seven new messages. The forum had twelve new replies. Someone had posted a link to an article he should read. It was forty pages long.

Outside, the night finished arriving. The orange glow stayed constant.

He started reading.

---

Merton wasn't at coffee the next Saturday, or the Saturday after that. Marcus knocked a few times, then stopped knocking. He was busy anyway. The revisions. The assessment portal. The forum. The email.

In April, he ran into Merton in the building's entrance. The older man was coming back from somewhere, canvas bag over his shoulder, looking wet.

"It was raining," Marcus said.

"I know."

"You didn't have an umbrella."

"Didn't need one." Merton shifted the bag. "Rain's free. Might as well enjoy it."

Marcus almost laughed. Enjoy standing in the rain. Like that was a thing people did. Like they had time for that.

"You look awful," Merton said.

"Thanks."

"I'm serious. When's the last time you slept?"

Marcus tried to remember. "I sleep."

"At 4 AM. Then wake up at seven. That's not sleeping. That's collapsing."

"I have work."

"You have a laptop."

"It's the same thing."

"Is it?"

Marcus's phone buzzed. Email. He pulled it out, glanced at the screen. Someone had finally responded to his essay submission. He needed to read it. He needed to read it now.

"I have to go," he said.

Merton watched him. The rain dripped from his jacket onto the tile floor. "You know what the difference is between a tool and a trap?"

Marcus waited.

"A tool," Merton said, "you put down when you're done. A trap keeps working whether you want it to or not."

"It's just a computer."

"So was Univac. So was HAL." Merton smiled, but again it didn't reach his eyes. "So's the portal you spent two hours on last week that accomplished nothing."

"How did you—"

"Mrs. Chen told me. She heard you swearing at it through the ceiling."

Marcus's face went hot. "I wasn't swearing at it."

"No. You were swearing at yourself for not understanding it. Which is worse."

The phone buzzed again. Another email. Marcus felt it in his pocket like a physical pull.

"I should go," he said.

"You should sleep," Merton said. "But you won't."

Upstairs, Marcus read the email. The essay was rejected. Different journal, same feedback: interesting ideas, needs tightening, the structure doesn't quite work.

He opened the document. Read the first paragraph. Saw the problems immediately. Started fixing them.

Three hours later, he was still fixing them. The document had grown by seven hundred words. None of them were right.

The laptop's glow filled the room. Outside, rain started again. He didn't hear it.

---

May arrived. Finals week. His students turned in final essays. Marcus graded them in marathon sessions, laptop open on the desk, coffee going cold in the cup. The papers were fine. Better than fine. They all said smart things. They all cited good sources. They all sounded exactly the same.

He gave them A's and B's and wondered if he'd actually taught them anything, or if he'd just taught them to search efficiently.

The semester ended. Summer stretched ahead: no classes, just research time. Three months to finish the revisions, get the essay published, maybe start something new.

He made a plan. Mornings: write. Afternoons: library. Evenings: off. Actual off, not working off.

The plan lasted four days.

On day five, he woke up at noon, foggy from six hours of sleep that felt like two. His laptop was still open on the desk, screen dark but warm to the touch. He'd put it to sleep at 4 AM. Or maybe 5. The clock had been swimming by then.

He checked his email. Forty messages. Half were spam. The other half required responses.

He checked the forum. Eighty-three new posts since last night.

He checked the journal. No response yet.

He checked the syllabus for fall semester. The department head wanted draft syllabi by June 15.

He checked the portal. The system wanted updated assessment data.

He checked, checked, checked. The laptop delivered everything he asked for and fifty things he hadn't. Each answer came wrapped in questions. Each question branched into three more.

By evening, he hadn't written anything. He'd read seventy pages about postwar drama, downloaded twelve PDFs, composed six forum replies, and spent ninety minutes trying to format a syllabus template the portal would accept.

The light outside faded to orange-gray. He turned on his desk lamp. The laptop stayed bright.

He thought: I'll stop at midnight.

At midnight, he thought: Just one more thing.

At 2 AM, his eyes burned and his neck ached and his brain felt like cotton, but he was close to something. He could feel it. One more search. One more source. One more—

The screen flickered. Battery warning. He plugged in the charger. The icon turned green. The screen brightened.

Outside, rain fell in steady sheets. Inside, the glow continued.

---

He saw Merton one more time, late June. Marcus was taking out trash around midnight, eyes gritty from eight hours of screen time. Merton was coming up from the basement, rain jacket on, heading for the door.

"Walk?" Marcus asked. It surprised him, asking.

Merton studied him. "Can you leave the phone?"

Marcus almost said no. Then he pulled it out, looked at it—black rectangle, screen smudged with fingerprints—and thought about the seventy-three unread notifications waiting inside. About the way his thumb moved to check it every few minutes, automatic as breathing. About how he'd started sleeping with it on the nightstand so he could check it first thing, last thing, middle thing when he woke at 3 AM from dreams where email notifications sounded like air raid sirens.

He put it on the lobby shelf next to the mailboxes. Merton nodded.

They walked. Past the campus, south toward the Point. The rain had stopped but everything was wet, the sidewalks glossy under streetlamps, the grass dark and heavy. The farther south they went, the fewer lights. By the time they reached the Point, the orange glow of the city was behind them. Ahead was just the lake, black water meeting blacker sky.

Merton stood at the edge, hands in pockets. Marcus stood beside him. The wind came off the water cold and clean.

"You see them?" Merton pointed up.

Marcus looked. For a moment, nothing. Then his eyes adjusted to the real dark—not the false night of the city, but actual darkness, the kind humans lived in for millennia before they invented streetlamps and porch lights and screens that glowed in empty rooms.

Stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

"I forgot," Marcus said.

"Everyone does."

They stood there. The water lapped at the rocks. A plane moved across the sky, red lights blinking.

"When I quit the department," Merton said, "everyone asked why. I told them I was tired. They thought I meant physically. But I wasn't tired from working. I was tired from never not working. Does that make sense?"

It did. It made terrible sense.

"The laptop is useful," Marcus said. "You can't deny that."

"I don't. The printing press was useful too. Doubled literacy rates, sparked the Reformation, changed the world. Also made it possible to print more propaganda, faster. Didn't stop being useful. Just turned out the usefulness came with a price."

"So what do you do? Just not use it?"

"I use the library. I read books. I write with a pen." Merton looked at him. "I sleep."

"I sleep."

"You survive. It's different."

Across the water, a foghorn sounded. Low and deep, rolling over the dark. Marcus felt it in his chest. When was the last time he'd heard something that wasn't filtered through speakers, pixels, compressed audio?

"The rain fell tonight," Merton said. "Did you notice?"

Marcus tried to remember. He'd been inside. Working. The rain must have started around eight. Or seven. Or maybe it had been raining all day and he hadn't looked out the window.

"No," he said.

"That's how it starts," Merton said. "First you stop noticing the rain. Then you stop noticing the daylight. Then one day you realize you've been living inside a glow for months, and you don't remember deciding to do it. You just... did."

The stars wheeled overhead. The water kept moving. Marcus thought about his apartment three miles north, his laptop sleeping on the desk, its charger plugged in, ready. Waiting. Always waiting.

"I have work tomorrow," he said.

"You have work every day."

"I know."

They walked back. The orange glow grew brighter with each block. Streetlamps, porch lights, traffic signals, store signs, apartment windows. The city's light bouncing off low clouds, erasing the stars.

At the building entrance, Marcus retrieved his phone. Ninety-one notifications. He thumbed it awake. The screen's glow painted his face blue-white.

"Thank you," he said to Merton. "For the walk."

"Will you do it again?"

Marcus looked at the notifications. Email, forum posts, messages. All of them waiting. All of them urgent. All of them more urgent tomorrow if he didn't handle them tonight.

"Probably not," he said.

Merton nodded. "That's honest, anyway."

Upstairs, Marcus sat at his desk. The laptop woke the moment he opened it. Seven tabs still there, exactly where he'd left them. Cursor blinking in a text field like a heartbeat.

He started typing.

---

By August, he'd stopped going outside except to teach. The essay revisions were done, submitted to a third journal. Waiting. While waiting, he'd started another piece. And joined another forum. And discovered a new archive that required exploring. And accepted an invitation to guest-post on someone's academic blog, which required research, which required searches, which required—

The work was good. Productive. He was producing. The laptop's hard drive had sixteen research folders, eighty-seven PDFs, and forty-three draft documents in various states of completion. That was progress. That was accomplishment. Numbers didn't lie.

His eyes hurt constantly now. He'd bought reading glasses even though he'd never needed glasses before. They helped. A little.

He'd lost weight. Not intentionally. Just forgot to eat sometimes. Or ate while working, staring at the screen, tasting nothing.

The department head had emailed about fall syllabi. He'd responded. Eventually. Maybe a week late. Or two weeks. She'd sent a follow-up. He'd meant to respond to the follow-up. That was yesterday. Or last week. The days had started blurring.

One morning—or afternoon, hard to tell, the curtains were closed—someone knocked. Marcus ignored it. The knocking continued. He stumbled to the door, opened it.

Merton stood there with a paper bag.

"Mrs. Kowalski made pierogi," he said. "Thought you might want some."

The smell hit Marcus: onions, potato, butter. His stomach cramped. When had he eaten? Yesterday. Maybe.

"Thanks," he said, taking the bag.

Merton looked past him into the apartment. Marcus followed his gaze: the laptop on the desk, glowing. Papers everywhere. Coffee mugs on every surface. The curtains drawn. The desk lamp on even though—Marcus checked—the clock said 2 PM.

"You okay?" Merton asked.

"I'm fine. Just busy."

"Busy." Merton said it flat.

"The semester starts soon. I have prep work."

"It's August 12. Semester starts September 5."

"Exactly. Not much time."

"Marcus—"

"I'm fine. Really. Thank you for the food."

He closed the door. Heard Merton's footsteps on the stairs, descending. Carried the bag to his desk, set it down next to the keyboard. The pierogi smelled incredible. He'd eat them later. After he finished this paragraph. After he checked the forum. After he—

The battery warning chimed. Low power. He plugged in the charger without looking, eyes already back on the screen.

The pierogi went cold on his desk. By the time he remembered them, it was past midnight. He ate them anyway, staring at the laptop while he chewed.

They didn't taste like anything.

---

Fall semester started. Marcus taught his classes. Held his office hours. Attended the department meetings. Smiled when he was supposed to smile. Nodded when he was supposed to nod.

All the while, a part of his mind was elsewhere. Thinking about the tabs still open on his laptop. The forum threads he hadn't finished. The responses he needed to write. The searches he needed to run. The work that was always waiting, patient and hungry, back in his apartment.

During a faculty workshop on inclusive pedagogy, he checked his phone under the table. Someone at the front was talking about learning outcomes. Someone always was.

His students' essays came in. He graded them. They all cited the same sources. They all made the same arguments. They were all good students, following instructions, doing the work, getting their A's and B's and moving forward. Just like he was.

In October, Merton's mailbox stayed full for a week. Then two weeks. Then Mrs. Chen mentioned in the mailroom that she'd seen a moving truck. Marcus felt something—not quite surprise, not quite loss.

Later that week, he passed 1A. The door was propped open. The bookshelves were gone. The desk was gone. Just empty walls and floor, the space looking smaller without furniture to define it. Someone new would move in. Someone who would bend the same way everyone else bent. Or not. It didn't matter.

That night, Marcus worked until 4 AM. The essay was still in revision. The third journal had rejected it. A fourth journal was considering it. While they considered, he'd started a new project. And joined another forum. And discovered a database that required exploring.

The laptop's glow filled the room. Outside, rain fell. He didn't hear it. Didn't see it. Just saw the screen, white and bright and endless, tab after tab after tab, each one a door to another room, another question, another search.

The battery was at 90%. The charger was plugged in. The searches could continue all night if he wanted.

He wanted.

His eyes burned. His neck ached. His brain felt full of cotton and static. Tomorrow he'd be useless in class. Tomorrow he'd be useless everywhere. But tomorrow wasn't now. Now was just the glow and the work and the endless scroll of information, useful and necessary and slowly, inevitably, quietly extracting something he couldn't quite name.

One more search, he thought. One more tab.

He opened it. The screen brightened. The cursor blinked, waiting.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Free and unnoticed. The city's orange glow bounced off clouds, erasing stars nobody looked for anymore.

Inside, Marcus kept working. The laptop stayed on. The night continued.

And somewhere in the distance, a foghorn sounded over dark water, heard by no one, marking nothing, calling to darkness that was already gone.
