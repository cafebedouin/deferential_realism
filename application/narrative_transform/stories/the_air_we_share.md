# The Air We Share

The counter on Linh's wrist clicked past 0400. She felt it more than heard it—a small vibration against skin that had learned to track time by oxygen cycles. Around her in the hab-pod, her family was already moving. Her mother Faduma strapping on boot weights for the early shift. Her younger brother Cumar still asleep in the upper bunk, his breathing shallow and quick the way everyone breathed after a few years domeside.

Linh pulled herself upright—too fast, always forgetting the 0.13g until she was halfway to the ceiling. She caught the handhold, settled back down. Through the viewport, Jupiter filled half the sky, its bands of cream and rust and that endless storm turning and turning. Three years she'd been watching that storm. Three years since they'd made the crossing.

"Anh-crew, you ready?" Faduma's voice carried the soft rasp that came after too many shifts in the plumes. She was checking the oxygen allocation on the wall panel, her fingers moving through the routine without looking. "Quota today is high. They pushing for export deadline."

Linh nodded, pulling on her thermal underlayer. The fabric was stiff where the sweat never quite dried in the recycled air. "Walaal still sleeping?"

"Let him. He not on rotation until midday." Faduma turned, and in the dim hab lighting her eyes had that distance Linh had started to see in everyone who'd been here longer than five years. Like they were looking at something just past your shoulder. "You remember check your hooyo-oxy before you go down?"

"Always do."

"Good girl." Faduma touched her shoulder, brief and warm, then moved toward the door. "See you at plaza for shaah tonight, yeah? If you get back before dim-cycle."

After she left, Linh sat on the edge of her bunk and checked her gear. Pressure suit. Helmet seal. The wrist monitor that tracked her air use down to the milliliter. The company said it was for safety. But safety was a funny word when they docked your family's rations if you breathed too deep during a shift.

She thought about the meeting in the sub-dome tonight. Thought about not thinking about it, which was the same thing. Wondered if her mother had noticed the extra hours she'd been logging in the old storage annex. Wondered if it mattered.

---

The descent shaft was crowded at shift change. Bodies in pressure suits, faces behind visors, everyone moving in the practiced shuffle that kept you from bouncing off the walls in low-g. Linh found herself next to Old Dinh, who'd been on Europa since the first wave in 2212. His hands shook when he held the handrails.

"You going down to the plumes?" His voice crackled through the suit comm, thick with the accent that mixed three languages into something new. "Water-mining bad today, I hear. Pressure fluctuations."

"I'm on collection crew." Linh kept her voice neutral. "Same as always."

Dinh's laugh turned into a cough. She could hear it rattling in his chest even through the comm static. "Same as always. That's the thing, không? Everything same. Your ba worked these plumes. Now you. Tomorrow maybe your little walaal."

"Maybe he'll do something different."

"Different." Another cough. "You young ones always saying that. Different. Like there something else here. You born to the ice, bé. You die to the ice. That's how it work."

The descent pod lurched, and they dropped toward the ocean layer beneath the crust. Through the tiny viewport, Linh watched the ice walls slide past—red and brown with bacterial growth, scarred from decades of mining operations. The sulfur smell was already seeping into her suit filters. By the end of shift it would taste like metal in the back of her throat for hours.

She didn't answer Dinh. There was no point. He'd stopped hearing about three years ago, around the same time his hands started shaking. The medic had a name for it—chronic hypoxia adaptation syndrome, neural degradation secondary to prolonged oxygen restriction. But everyone just called it dome-brain. The thing that happened when you'd been breathing thin air for too long, when your body had rewired itself around survival and forgotten how to think about anything else.

The pod shuddered to a stop. Depth gauge read 2.3 kilometers beneath the ice.

"Okay, crew," the shift coordinator's voice came through the general channel. "Standard collection protocol. Plume activity is elevated in sector seven, so we stick to the approved zones. Nobody tries anything creative. The consortium needs these quotas met, and creative gets people hurt."

Linh sealed her helmet and checked her air: eight hours at standard consumption, six if she had to work hard. The counter on her wrist started its quiet tick-tick-tick.

---

The work was exactly what it had been yesterday, and the day before that, and the 847 days before that since she'd turned sixteen and joined the rotation. Guide the collection tubes into the mineral-rich plumes erupting from the ocean floor. Monitor the flow rates. Watch for pressure spikes that meant a tube was about to rupture and fill your suit with superheated brine. Do it again. Do it again. Do it again.

Her mind went where it always went when her hands knew the routine too well.

The sub-dome collective. Six of them now, meeting in the abandoned annex after shifts when the consortium monitors were focused on production zones. Mai and Rahma and Yusuf and Hassan and Kenji and herself. All of them born domeside. All of them gene-tweaked in the 2230s adaptation program—enhanced neural plasticity, radiation resistance, improved pattern recognition. The consortium had wanted workers who could handle Europa better. They'd gotten something else.

They'd gotten kids who could feel the dome in their heads.

It had started small. Mai had figured out how to link her implant to the hydroponic monitors, reading nutrient flows without accessing the panel. Then Hassan had synced with her, and suddenly they could balance the tanks together, making tiny adjustments in real-time that the automated systems missed. The plants grew faster. Used less water. Produced more oxygen.

They'd kept it quiet at first. But you couldn't hide that kind of efficiency. The coordinator had noticed. Logged it. Sent a report up the chain.

Two days later, the medic had shown up at Mai's hab asking questions about "neurological anomalies" and "unauthorized implant modifications." Asking in that careful way that meant the company already knew the answers and was deciding what to do about them.

That's when they'd moved to the annex. When they'd started calling it the collective. When they'd stopped pretending they were just trying to help with the hydroponics.

Because once you could sync with one person, you could sync with two. With three. With all of them at once, thoughts moving between minds like water between vessels. Not telepathy—the biologists in the old world would have had better words for it. Distributed cognition. Neural networking. Emergent group intelligence.

But on Europa, in the recycled air and the metal-tang darkness, it felt like something simpler than that.

It felt like not being alone inside your own head for the first time in your life.

A pressure alarm jolted her back. Her collection tube had drifted into a thermal vent, and the reading was spiking toward red. Linh jerked it back, realigned, watched the numbers settle. The counter on her wrist had barely moved. Only twenty minutes into the shift.

Six hours and forty minutes to go.

---

The plaza after shift was always the same. People too tired to do anything but sit, but too wired from the fear and the thin air to actually rest. The tea circles had already formed—Faduma was there with her group, passing cups of shaah that smelled like home even if the spices came from algae derivatives and hope. The Vietnamese elders had their pho corner, steam rising from bowls that held more memory than nutrition.

Linh took her bowl from old Grandmother Hoa and found a spot against the wall where she could watch without being watched. Her body was here, making the motions of normal. Her mind was already counting down until the annex meeting.

"You look far away, bé." Grandmother Hoa settled next to her with the slow care of someone who'd lived through low-g for thirty-five years. "Far like you thinking about something big."

"Just tired."

"Tired is one thing. But you got that look. The one your mother had when she first got here. Like you seeing something past the ice walls." Hoa sipped her pho, made a small sound of contentment. "You be careful with that look. Consortium notices when people start seeing past things."

Linh turned the bowl in her hands. The warmth felt good, but the algae noodles tasted like nothing much. Three years domeside and she'd forgotten what real pho was supposed to taste like. Or maybe that was just the dome-brain starting, the way flavors went flat and memories got fuzzy around the edges and everything became just survive-survive-survive.

"What if there was different?" The words came out before she could stop them. "What if we didn't have to just... keep doing the same thing?"

Hoa was quiet for a long time. Then: "Different how?"

Linh thought about the annex. About six minds moving together, solving problems in minutes that would take one person hours. About the new hydroponic design they'd worked out, the one that could cut water usage by forty percent and increase yield at the same time. About how when they were linked, the fog in her head would clear and she could think the way she used to think back before the air got thin.

"Just different," she said.

"Mm." Hoa finished her pho, set the bowl aside with careful hands. "You know what I think? I think the dome already decided what different looks like. And it looks like this. It looks like shaah in the plaza and work in the plumes and your babies growing up to work in the plumes. That's the shape of the world here."

"But what if—"

"No what-ifs, bé. What-ifs get you extended contracts. What-ifs get you flagged in the medical reports. What-ifs get your family's air allocation reviewed." Hoa's voice was gentle, but there was something underneath it that made Linh's chest tight. "I seen a lot of what-ifs over thirty-five years. They all end the same way."

---

The annex meeting started at 2200, during the gap between shifts when the monitors were least attentive. Linh waited until Faduma was asleep—the soft snoring that meant real rest, not just dome-brain exhaustion—before slipping out of the hab-pod.

The annex was in the old storage section, where the first construction crews had left equipment that never got used once the permanent facilities were built. The consortium had sealed it off for safety, but Hassan's father had been on that first crew, and Hassan had kept his access codes. They'd turned it into something else. Something theirs.

Mai was already there when Linh arrived, her neural implant flickering with tiny lights at her temple. "We're all here except Kenji. He's running late—his shift coordinator held everyone for a quota lecture."

"Again?" Rahma shook her head. She was the oldest of them, twenty-two, and sometimes Linh could see the fog starting around her eyes too. "They've been pushing numbers all week. Export deadline must be close."

"Closer than that." Yusuf had the monitoring data pulled up on a jury-rigged screen. "I accessed the shipping schedules. Big transport leaves in six days. They're trying to meet the quarterly targets."

Which meant everyone would be pushed harder. More hours, less rest, quotas climbing until someone made a mistake. Until a pressure seal failed or a plume erupted wrong or someone just got too tired and forgot to check their air and—

Linh pushed the thought away. "Should we wait for Kenji?"

"No. Let's start." Mai closed her eyes, and Linh felt the first touch of the link opening. Like a hand reaching out in darkness. Like finding a door in a wall you'd thought was solid.

She let herself sink into it.

The annex disappeared. Or didn't disappear—became layered, somehow, with other perspectives. She could feel Mai's awareness of the hydroponic flows, Rahma's intimate knowledge of the life-support cycling rates, Hassan's understanding of the structural supports and pressure differentials. Yusuf's careful tracking of every resource allocation, every quota, every debt compounding in the consortium's credit ledgers.

And underneath it all, the thing they didn't talk about but all knew: how much clearer their minds were when they were linked. How the fog lifted. How they could think again the way they were supposed to think, before the thin air and the fear rewired their brains around nothing but making it through the next shift.

*This is what we could be,* Mai thought, and her thought was everyone's thought. *This is what they took from us.*

*We could run the whole dome this way,* from Hassan. *Optimize everything. Cut the waste. Make the quotas without the killing pace.*

*They'd never allow it,* from Yusuf, and his thought carried the weight of all those spreadsheets, all those numbers that said exactly who owned what and how much air you were permitted and what happened when you tried to change the terms. *We're tools. Tools don't get to reorganize the workshop.*

*We're not tools,* from Linh, fierce and sudden. *We're people. We're here and we're breathing and we're thinking and we're—*

The door to the annex opened.

The link shattered. Linh's mind slammed back into her own skull with a force that made her gasp. Mai cried out. Around the room, everyone was reeling, disconnected, alone inside themselves again.

Dr. Pham stood in the doorway. The domeside medic. Consortium-trained, consortium-loyal, but there was something in her face that Linh couldn't read.

"I need to talk to you," Dr. Pham said quietly. "All of you. Right now."

---

They sat in the annex, separated again, while Dr. Pham pulled up files on her datapad. Medical files. Neurological scans. Reports flagged with red headers that meant "review required" in consortium code.

"I've been tracking the adaptation syndrome cases," she said. "The cognitive degradation. Everyone calls it dome-brain, but the technical term is—" She stopped herself. "It doesn't matter what we call it. What matters is what causes it."

"The air," Rahma said. "Low oxygen. Over time it—"

"No." Dr. Pham's voice was flat. "I mean, yes, the hypoxia is a factor. But that's not what causes the neural fog. That's not what makes people forget how to think about anything except surviving the next shift."

She turned the datapad around. Showed them scans of brain activity—before and after images that Linh didn't fully understand but could read the shapes of. Neural pathways lighting up, then dimming. Structures changing. Rewiring.

"The gene modifications from the adaptation program," Dr. Pham continued. "The radiation resistance, the enhanced neural plasticity—they work. They work too well. Your brains are adaptive. They optimize for the environment they're in. And when that environment is chronic stress, chronic fear, chronic resource scarcity..." She trailed off. "Your brain learns that thinking about the future is a waste of energy. That creative problem-solving is a luxury you can't afford. That the only thing that matters is getting through today."

Mai's voice was barely a whisper. "So we're... we're breaking ourselves? Just by being here?"

"Your bodies are trying to save you. By making you stop wanting anything except survival." Dr. Pham closed the files. "The ones without the modifications—like your parents—they just get the hypoxia damage. But they still think like they used to. They still want things. They still hope."

"And we don't?" Hassan asked.

"You do. But every day, a little less. Every shift, your brain prunes away the parts that aren't about immediate survival. Within five years, you'll be just like Old Dinh. Just like all the first-generation adapted workers. Efficient. Focused. Dead inside except for the parts that know how to harvest plumes and avoid pressure spikes and make quota."

The annex was very quiet.

"But when we link," Linh said slowly. "When we're together, it's different. We can think. We can—"

"I know." Dr. Pham met her eyes. "I've been monitoring it. The distributed processing offloads the survival anxiety. Spreads it across multiple brains so no single one is overwhelmed. When you're linked, you're not just surviving. You're actually problem-solving. Creating. Thinking about tomorrow instead of just today."

"So let us do it," Mai said, and there was something desperate in her voice. "Let us link more. Build it out. Show the consortium how much more efficient we could be if—"

"I can't."

The words dropped like a pressure seal failure. Final. Absolute.

"I've been ordered to file a report," Dr. Pham continued. "On neurological anomalies in the adapted worker population. On unauthorized use of neural implants. On..." She stopped. Started again. "On potential risks to operational stability."

"Risks," Yusuf said flatly.

"You're talking to each other in ways the consortium can't monitor. You're optimizing systems in ways that might reduce long-term labor requirements. You're..." Dr. Pham's hands were shaking. Just slightly. Just enough to see. "You're thinking about what comes after the quotas. And that makes you dangerous."

Linh felt something cold settle in her chest. "So they'll shut us down."

"If I file the report, yes. The oversight board will review it. They'll decide that the neural linking represents a category-three adaptation—unauthorized emergent capabilities. They'll recommend termination of the implant modifications. Reversal surgery if possible. Isolation protocols if not."

"And you're going to file it," Hassan said. Not a question.

Dr. Pham looked at each of them in turn. When she got to Linh, something in her expression cracked. "I have to. I signed a contract. I took their passage, their training, their equipment. They own my work for another eight years. If I don't file, they'll know. They monitor everything. And then it won't just be you they isolate."

"So we're already gone," Rahma said quietly.

"I'm sorry."

"How long do we have?" Mai asked.

"I have to submit by end of week. Five days." Dr. Pham stood, and she looked smaller than Linh had ever seen her. "I'm telling you now because—because you deserve to know. Because maybe you can... I don't know. Prepare. Hide it better. Something."

After she left, the six of them sat in the silence of the annex, each trapped inside their own heads again. The link was still there—Linh could feel it, like a door she could open if she wanted. But she didn't open it. None of them did.

Because they all knew what they'd find if they linked now. They'd find the same thing. The same realization spreading across six connected minds until it was so heavy that no one could carry it.

That there was nothing to prepare for. Nothing to hide. That the world they lived in had a shape, and they didn't fit inside it, and the world didn't care.

---

Linh went to her shift the next morning because there was nowhere else to go. Guided the collection tubes. Watched the pressure gauges. Felt the counter on her wrist tick-tick-tick through the hours of air she had left.

The collective met twice more in the annex. But they didn't link. They talked about options that weren't really options. About what would happen if they just stopped using the implants. About what would happen if they ran.

But running meant no air. Meant the vacuum and the cold and Jupiter's radiation. Meant dying in hours instead of years.

So they talked. And then they stopped talking. And then they stopped meeting.

On the fourth night, Linh went to the plaza and sat with Grandmother Hoa. The old woman didn't ask questions. Just poured tea and watched Jupiter turn through its endless day.

"I saw Dr. Pham earlier," Hoa said eventually. "She looked tired. Sad tired, not shift tired."

Linh sipped her shaah. It tasted like metal and algae and the memory of cardamom.

"She doing a report on the adapted workers," Hoa continued, her voice casual in that careful way that meant she knew more than she was saying. "On the ones doing the link thing. The ones trying to be different."

"You knew."

"Everyone knows, bé. We're all breathing the same air in here. There no such thing as secrets when the walls are three meters apart." Hoa set down her cup. "You think we don't see? Don't understand?"

"Then why didn't you—"

"Because understanding and changing are not the same thing." The old woman's eyes were distant, looking at something past the ice walls. "Your ba understood. My son understood. All the first wave understood. But understanding doesn't fix the air. Doesn't change the contracts. Doesn't make the consortium decide different."

"We could have helped," Linh said, and hated how young her voice sounded. "With the linking. We could have optimized everything. Made it better for everyone."

"I know."

"Then why—"

"Because better for everyone means fewer workers needed. Means automated systems replace manual crews. Means contracts get terminated early without passage home." Hoa's voice was gentle. "Means people like your mother, like Old Dinh, like me—we become obsolete. And obsolete people don't get air allocation on Europa."

Linh stared at her shaah. Watched the surface shimmer in the dim plaza light.

"So we all just... stay the same. Forever. Until the dome-brain takes everything and we're just—"

"Just alive." Hoa touched her hand. "I'm sorry, bé. I know you thought you could change the shape of the world. But the world is the world. And we are what we are inside it."

---

Dr. Pham filed her report on the fifth day. Linh heard about it through the whisper network that moved faster than official channels—Mai's mother worked in the admin section and had seen the document flagged for oversight review.

The next morning, all six of them were summoned to the medical bay.

The consortium overseer was there. A woman named Chen who Linh had only ever seen on screens before, giving quota updates from the orbital station. In person, she was smaller than expected. Tired-looking. Human.

"We've reviewed Dr. Pham's findings," Chen said without preamble. "The unauthorized neural linking represents a significant operational concern. However, the oversight board recognizes that the adaptation program was our initiative. We're not interested in punishment."

Mai's hand found Linh's, squeezed hard.

"The implant modifications will be reversed," Chen continued. "The procedure is minimally invasive. You'll be cleared for shift work within three days. All records of the linking experiments will be sealed. This doesn't go on your files or affect your families' contracts."

"And if we refuse?" Hassan's voice was steady.

"Then it goes on your files as a category-three violation. Your families lose current contract terms and renegotiate at new rates." Chen's expression didn't change. "We'd prefer not to do that. The rates are not favorable."

Of course they weren't. Linh knew without asking that "not favorable" meant years added to contracts. Meant debt compounding at rates that would trap entire families for generations. Meant making an example out of six kids who'd thought they could find a different shape for the world.

"We'll do it," Rahma said quietly. "The reversal."

Chen nodded like she'd never doubted it. "Good. Scheduling will send you appointment times. Dr. Pham will handle the procedures."

After she left, they sat in the medical bay and didn't look at each other. Linh tried to open the link—one last time, just to feel that clarity again—but Mai shook her head.

"Don't. If we link now..." She didn't finish. Didn't need to.

If they linked now, they'd feel it together. Would share the weight of it across six connected minds. And that would make it real in a way that sitting here in silence couldn't.

---

The reversal surgery took forty minutes. Linh woke up with a pressure headache and a small bandage at her temple where the implant had been modified. Dr. Pham checked her vitals, made notes on a datapad, didn't meet her eyes.

"Side effects should be minimal," the doctor said. "Some disorientation for a few days. Maybe some memory gaps around the linking experiences—that's normal when we interrupt established neural pathways. But you should be cleared for shift work by tomorrow."

"That's it?" Linh heard her own voice like it was coming from very far away. "Just... back to normal?"

"Yes."

"And the dome-brain thing? The cognitive degradation?"

Dr. Pham was quiet for a long time. Then: "That's still happening. The reversal just removes the linking capability. It doesn't change the underlying adaptation syndrome."

"So we'll still forget. Still stop wanting things. Still become like Old Dinh."

"Yes."

Linh lay back on the med-bay bed and stared at the ceiling. Counted the rivets in the metal panels. Tried to feel something about it and couldn't quite remember what feeling was supposed to feel like.

Maybe the dome-brain was starting faster than she'd thought.

---

Three months later, Linh guided collection tubes in the plumes and thought about nothing much. The work was the same as always. The counter on her wrist ticked through hours of air. Jupiter turned through its endless day above the ice.

Sometimes she saw Mai in the plaza, or Hassan in the descent shaft, or the others scattered through the dome doing their shifts and drinking their shaah and breathing their allocated air. They didn't talk about the annex anymore. Didn't talk about linking or optimization or being different.

Mostly they didn't talk at all.

Faduma said it was maturity. Said Linh was learning to accept the shape of the world. Said that was good, that it meant she was adapting.

Old Dinh said everyone got quieter after a few years domeside. Said it was just the air getting thin, the way it always did.

Grandmother Hoa didn't say anything. Just poured tea and watched and knew.

And Linh worked her shifts and ate her algae and tried to remember what it had felt like to think about tomorrow. But tomorrow was hard to picture when today took all your energy just to survive. And after a while, she stopped trying.

After a while, she stopped noticing she'd stopped.

The dome had its shape, and she fit inside it now, and that was enough.

It had to be enough.

It was all there was.
